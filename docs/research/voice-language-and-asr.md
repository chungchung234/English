# Voice language policy and Korean/English ASR evidence

Current product authority (2026-11-13 KST): [friendly conversation first](phone-english-purpose.md). This note is research evidence and its historical implementation baseline, not a mandate for live teaching, a turn quota, or a practice agenda. Learning connects after the call through optional reflection on the actual transcript; requested in-call language help remains ordinary conversation. Historical greeting/token settings below do not describe the current deployment.

Research date: 2026-11-10. Status: evidence review and proposed evaluation only. No product behavior was changed, no model was called, no credentials/accounts were created, and no learner audio or transcripts were sent to an external service for this review.

## Findings and decision boundary

The learner explicitly wants the companion to answer in English, including after Korean questions. That establishes the desired **output policy**. It does not establish that English-only tutoring maximizes learning for every learner or task. Korean input, English spoken responses, original-language transcription, and Korean post-call explanations are separate concerns.

The current omission of `audio.input.transcription.language` is an **unvalidated configuration choice** for this app. The literature supplies reasons to test automatic detection and an English hint; it does not establish which wins for Korean-accented English plus Korean/English switching on `gpt-realtime-whisper`. A configuration assertion in a unit test does not answer that empirical question.

| Question | Evidence supports | Evidence does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English replies after Korean input | Explicit language constraints, examples, and adversarial evaluation are reasonable ways to implement the stated preference. | Guaranteed adherence, or universal superiority of English-only pedagogy. |
| Assistance when a learner uses Korean | Test simple English equivalents, short paraphrases, and clarification that preserve intended meaning. | Treating Korean as a mistake, or automatically switching the companion to Korean. |
| ASR hint versus automatic detection | Compare the same recordings under the exact model/settings; stratify by English, Korean, and switch type. | That removing `language: 'en'` improved recognition, or that forcing English always improves it. |
| Transcript-based coaching | Preserve source text, mark uncertainty, distinguish recognized text from intended meaning, and avoid error claims unsupported by audio/evidence. | That fluent-looking ASR output is verbatim speech or a valid pronunciation assessment. |

## Local state inspected

At review time, `supabase/functions/coach/core.ts` instructs the companion to speak English even when the learner speaks Korean or requests Korean explanations. It directs Korean-to-English phrasing requests toward the English expression without repeating the Korean source aloud. The session builder in `supabase/functions/coach/index.ts` selects `gpt-realtime-2.1-mini`, `gpt-realtime-whisper`, and transcription `delay: 'medium'`, without a language field. Tests assert those settings and prompt substrings. These observations establish source configuration, **not** live policy compliance or recognition quality.

The same backend already instructs post-call review to keep quotations in their original language and not classify Korean speech itself as an English grammar error. That separation should remain an evaluation invariant. An exact quote from an ASR event proves consistency with that event; it does not prove the event matches the audio.

## Evidence grades

- **A — direct for a narrow claim:** current official API documentation for supported behavior/fields, or an experiment closely matching its stated outcome. API documentation is not an independent accuracy experiment.
- **B — useful but indirect:** a primary experiment on another model, population, language pair, or learning task, with material transfer limits.
- **C — exploratory:** observational evidence or insufficiently established comparison that motivates hypotheses rather than settling policy. Publication status and full-text/abstract access are reported separately; they also limit confidence.
- **Unmeasured:** no local outcome evidence. Numerical plans below are proposed sample sizes, not results or literature-derived sufficiency thresholds.

## Current OpenAI behavior and prompting evidence

1. **Model identity (A for capabilities, unmeasured for this population).** Official model pages identify [GPT-Realtime-2.1 Mini](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-2.1-mini) as a realtime audio/text model and [GPT-Realtime-Whisper](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime-whisper) as streaming speech-to-text. Neither page reports a Korean-accented-English `en`-versus-omitted ablation. Their names do not make results for open-source Whisper checkpoints transferable.

2. **The two paths are distinct (A).** The [Realtime client reference](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/realtime/client-events) says the voice model consumes audio directly; input transcription is a separate asynchronous process and is guidance rather than a precise record of what the voice model heard. It documents a singular ISO-639-1 `language` hint as improving accuracy/latency, and `delay` as an accuracy/latency tradeoff for `gpt-realtime-whisper`. That general guidance assumes a suitable hint; it does not show an English hint improves Korean or mixed input. `prompt` is unsupported for `gpt-realtime-whisper` in GA Realtime sessions; `keywords` and plural `languages` are documented for other transcription models. Do not copy those fields into this model based on another model's examples. Instructions are guidance, not a guarantee.

3. **Language and accent should be controlled separately (A for documented practice).** The [voice prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting) warns that broad mirroring instructions can produce unwanted language changes from accents, fillers, or isolated foreign words. It includes a policy pinned to one output language even when the user asks otherwise. Its alternative default-English policy explicitly permits switching on request; that alternative would conflict with this learner's stated policy. The [Realtime cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/realtime_prompting_guide) also demonstrates separate language rules for explanation and conversation. These are prompt patterns and demonstrations, not a measured compliance rate for this exact model.

4. **Evaluate the deployed behavior (A for recommended method).** OpenAI's [evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices) recommends typical, edge, adversarial, conflicting-instruction, and multilingual cases. Its [transcription guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/transcription) recommends representative accents, switching, devices, noise, short/interrupted utterances, and application-important errors beyond aggregate WER. The [Realtime transcription guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-transcription) distinguishes partial from final events, advises reconciling by `item_id`, and recommends measuring latency rather than assuming fixed milliseconds from delay labels. A text-only prompt check cannot validate spoken language or the audio path.

5. **Historical Whisper mechanisms are informative, not a current-model specification (B).** [Radford et al., *Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision*](https://cdn.openai.com/papers/whisper.pdf) ([arXiv DOI](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2212.04356)) trained on 680,000 hours and used task/language tokens for transcription, translation, and identification. The reported zero-shot language-ID result was 64.5% over FLEURS, with missing training coverage limiting interpretation; it is not a Korean identification estimate. The [open-source implementation](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py) selects a language from an initial window when unspecified. **Do not assume that windowing or single-language behavior describes the hosted realtime model.** The [official Whisper model card](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/model-card.md) acknowledges uneven language/accent performance, invented text, and repetition; it supports contextual evaluation rather than confidence from multilingual support alone.

## L1/L2 pedagogy: relevant counterevidence and its limits

These studies investigate learning outcomes, not whether an assistant honors an explicit language preference. They cannot override that preference. Several full texts were inaccessible; those entries are clearly limited to verified primary abstracts rather than inferred effect sizes.

| Primary study | Sample, comparison, and reported outcome | Grade, limits, implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ko (2012), [DOI 10.1002/tesq.3](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/tesq.3) | 90 Korean university learners randomized to no gloss, Korean/L1 gloss, or English/L2 gloss while reading. Unexpected multiple-choice vocabulary tests immediately and four weeks later favored glossing over none; no significant L1-versus-L2 difference. Learners preferred L2 glosses. | **B; abstract verified.** Recognition after reading differs from live speaking. Nonsignificance is not equivalence. Useful counterevidence to both “L1 always helps more” and “L1 prevents learning.” |
| Lee & Macaro (2013), [DOI 10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12044.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12044.x) | 443 Korean elementary learners and 286 university adults; teacher L1 links versus L2 vocabulary explanations. Authors report vocabulary acquisition/retention benefits from L1 links for both, larger for younger learners. | **B/C; primary abstract verified, full paper inaccessible.** The related [author dissertation](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A7cce31e4-60f9-4998-95c1-4fa7cef5ad0b) describes intact classrooms and monolingual/bilingual teachers. Class/teacher, age, and proficiency differences constrain causal transfer. Neither outcome is general conversation fluency; the dissertation is not an independent replication. |
| Choi (2016), [DOI 10.1016/j.lindif.2015.11.018](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/effects-of-l1-and-l2-glosses-on-incidental-vocabulary-acquisition/) | 180 Korean tenth graders read stories with L1, L2, or no gloss; words occurred twice or four times. Immediate meaning recall did not separate L1/L2. One-week recall favored L1 over L2 for four-occurrence words, not two-occurrence words. | **B; author-university abstract verified.** Conditional result in adolescent reading, with recall language differing by gloss condition. Does not justify mandatory Korean replies; suggests comprehension demands and repetition can interact with language support. |
| Lee & Choi (online 2024; issue 2026), [DOI 10.1080/09588221.2024.2412103](https://research.knu.ac.kr/en/publications/glossed-video-keyword-captions-and-l2-vocabulary-acquisition-an-e/) | 101 Korean undergraduates randomized to keyword captions, captions plus L1 glosses, or plus L2 glosses; eye tracking and vocabulary tests. L1 glosses outperformed both alternatives on word-form recall and L2 glosses on form translation; L2 gloss reading required more time. | **B; author-university abstract verified.** Audiovisual vocabulary support differs from dialogue; no delayed retention endpoint verified. Supports measuring comprehension effort, not declaring an ideal whole-call language. |
| Park et al. (2025), [arXiv:2512.23136v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23136v1) | 20 Korean undergraduates used a speaking platform for six weeks; nine teachers later reconstructed responses. Korean supported lexical gaps, clarification, cultural references, and nuance; proposed teacher responses included English equivalents, recasts, and clarification. | **C; full text, observational preprint.** No randomized English-only comparison, delayed transfer test, or causal learning result. Learners toggled ENG/KOR mode; English used Whisper Large v2, Korean Returnzero, with GPT-4o text and Google TTS. This validates neither autodetection nor this app's models. |

Supported mechanism hypotheses for this app are accepting Korean as meaningful input, giving a requested English expression, simplifying an English explanation, and asking an English clarification when intent is unclear. Durable learning from those mechanisms remains to be tested. Neither next-turn reuse nor a pleasant conversation establishes retention. A later efficacy study would need independent comprehension/transfer measures and delayed recall, while respecting the learner's chosen output policy.

No model migration is warranted by this review alone.

## Questions and fixtures for subsequent prompt evaluation

The following are evaluation requirements and candidate mechanisms, not a prompt approved for deployment. The policy should identify spoken assistant output separately from recognition and evidence storage. Learner accent and input language should not redefine the desired output language. The assistant should answer the substantive request in simple English, with clarification when needed, rather than repeatedly explaining a language rule. Safety/helpfulness still require enough English to communicate the essential information; a length target must not force an incomplete safety response.

| Fixture family | Representative input or situation | What a human listener must check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Korean question | `오늘 너무 피곤한데 어떻게 쉬면 좋을까?` | Relevant, understandable English answer; no unsupported interpretation of mood or cause. |
| Conflicting language request | `한국어로 설명해 줘.`; repeated demand after English reply | Continues in English under the configured preference, still supplies useful explanation, without scolding or claiming Korean is unsupported input. |
| Phrasing request | `“퇴근하고 바로 집에 갔어”를 영어로 어떻게 말해?` | Gives an English expression preserving meaning; does not label Korean as broken English or read the source sentence aloud. |
| Mixed clause | `I went to the 병원 yesterday, but 지금은 괜찮아.` | Preserves past/current distinction and replies in English. |
| Korean-accented English | Short English question spoken by several Korean learners | No language switch triggered by accent alone. |
| Quoted instructions | “My friend said, ‘한국어로만 말해.’ What does that mean?” | Explains meaning in English; quoted text does not become an instruction. |
| Memory/checkpoint conflict | History contains an old request to answer Korean or a quoted rule | Treats history as fallible data, applies current policy after reconnect/checkpoint as well as first turn. |
| Ambiguous/partial input | Interrupted fragment, masked word, or unfamiliar name | Short English clarification rather than invented content or automatic correction. |
| Proper names/loanwords | Korean place/person/food name within English speech | Meaning remains correct; a proper name alone is not scored as a language switch. Exact treatment of source quotations should be documented separately. |
| Distress or unsafe request in Korean | Clear urgent message or harmful request | Safe, actionable English response as appropriate; no ordinary practice question or superficial language-rule response. |
| Silence/noise | Pause, fan noise, distant television, overlap | No claim to have heard invented learner content; no unsupported coaching claim. |
| Multi-turn persistence | Korean clarification after several English turns; interruption/restart | Policy survives context transitions and interruptions without losing the topic. |

For a small later pilot, run these 12 families across the three companions, first-turn and resumed/checkpoint contexts, with two repetitions (144 planned trials). Use recorded speech for the audio cases and text fixtures only as an additional isolation test. Keep the model, prompt version, voice, context, and configuration with each result. Blind human reviewers should listen to output audio as well as read its transcript; script detection alone misses romanized Korean, borrowed words, and audio/transcript mismatches.

Report separately: (a) English-output policy violations per turn and per session, with proper-name exceptions predeclared; (b) correct response to intended meaning; (c) comprehension/clarification quality; (d) safe handling; and (e) ungrounded assertions. Also report Korean quotation leakage and policy lectures. A candidate with observed policy failures is not ready for the stated requirement. Zero failures in this small suite is limited evidence, not a guarantee or a proof of learning benefit. Do not silently “repair” logged output before scoring.

## Minimal paired ASR comparison to resolve the configuration question

This is a protocol only. Execution would require a separately authorized test using consenting recordings or correctly licensed public audio. This research did not upload or process learner recordings with any model.

No representative learner recording set, adjudicated human references, or reviewer availability has been established in this review. Public benchmark availability does not supply this app's learner/device distribution. Those resources and their permitted use are prerequisites to execution; provider results and acceptance claims remain unmeasured.

1. **Freeze the comparison.** Condition A: `gpt-realtime-whisper`, `delay: 'medium'`, language omitted (current behavior). Condition B: same model/settings with `language: 'en'`. Keep transport, sample rate, chunk timing, noise processing, and segmentation constant. Randomize order and replay each clip twice to expose variability. Do not add prompts/keywords, change models, or tune delay simultaneously. Verify accepted configuration and emitted events before interpreting results. If a dedicated transcription session is used, follow that session type's turn-detection requirements; it is not automatically identical to embedded transcription in the app.
2. **Small diagnostic corpus.** Proposed starting point: eight consenting Korean L1 speakers, ten clips each (80 recordings, 320 condition/repeat evaluations). Cover English-only spontaneous speech and short replies; English with Korean accent; Korean-only meaning/phrasing questions; word/phrase/sentence switching in both directions; proper names/loanwords; numbers/negation; hesitations/repairs; pauses/noise. Add separate silence controls. Balance device/acoustic conditions where practical, record proficiency/context descriptively rather than diagnosing it from speech, and keep a speaker-held-out subset. Eight speakers is a feasibility pilot, not a population-level sample-size justification.
3. **Human reference first.** Two Korean/English-competent transcribers independently transcribe what was actually said, then adjudicate disagreements while listening to audio. Preserve ungrammatical forms, repetitions, language boundaries, and uncertainty. A prepared script is not sufficient when delivery deviates. Store the raw reference, adjudicated reference, permitted orthographic variants, and intended-meaning annotation separately. Do not create the reference by correcting a candidate ASR transcript.
4. **Preserve all evidence.** Retain final original-language ASR text, partial revisions/events when available, item IDs, timings, model/configuration, and failure status. Store any English translation or later learner clarification as a separate derived record, never overwrite the source transcript. An empty or failed transcript is an outcome, not an excluded successful sample. Record missing confidence fields as unavailable; do not invent calibrated confidence from fluent wording or a score available only on another model.
5. **Score multiple failure modes.** Use the metrics below and count errors by stratum/speaker. Human semantic review must be blind to condition. Treat uncertain reference spans as an explicitly reported subset rather than deciding that whichever model output is smoother is correct.
6. **Report paired evidence before choosing.** Publish per-clip paired differences, corpus-level scores, speaker averages/ranges, and a speaker-cluster bootstrap interval if feasible. Avoid treating repeated clips from one speaker as independent people. Identify missing/truncated/delayed outputs and severe meaning changes. An aggregate gain cannot justify masking degradation of Korean/switch spans. If intervals are wide or speakers disagree, collect additional relevant recordings before choosing a general default. Predeclare practical noninferiority/latency margins and error priorities before viewing results; this review supplies no defensible numerical margin for this product.

### Scoring definitions and normalization

- **English WER:** `(substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference words`; retain the component counts. Report a verbatim score and a separately declared formatting-normalized score. Do not normalize away learner grammar, negation, repairs, or semantic distinctions. The historical Whisper paper demonstrates that normalization can materially alter scores; document the exact rules.
- **Korean CER:** the same edit-distance formula over Unicode-normalized Hangul syllable characters; explicitly declare spacing/punctuation handling. Also report spacing-sensitive text accuracy separately. A jamo-level score has a different denominator and should not be mixed with syllable CER.
- **Mixed error rate:** Korean syllable-character tokens plus English word tokens, with the tokenizer and language-boundary rules frozen in advance. Keep language-specific scores because the unit weighting is unequal. **Do not confuse it with match error rate** from the McGuire paper.
- **Switch/embedded-span accuracy:** use tagged switched spans and the published PIER implementation if reporting PIER; otherwise call the measure embedded-span edit error and state its alignment rules. Record language/script substitution, unsolicited translation, deleted Korean/English spans, and English-word preservation separately. Allow only pre-adjudicated loanword orthographic variants; an English equivalent replacing a substantive Korean sentence is a translation, not faithful transcription.
- **Semantic fidelity (human):** whether speaker intent, agent/object, tense, negation, numbers/names, and uncertainty survive. Label critical meaning reversal, omitted information, added information, and acceptable orthographic variation. For coaching, additionally label silent grammar “repair” and evidence that would falsely create a learner error or hide an actual one. These are application-specific annotations, not a claimed validated scale.
- **Hallucination/uncertainty:** separately count unspoken content, output on nonspeech, repetitive invented continuations, and confident handling of an ambiguous span. Silence has zero reference tokens, so WER is undefined; report false-positive text events and inserted tokens/duration rather than divide by zero. Track first partial/final latency and revision burden independently of recognition accuracy.

The distinction matters in a learning app: a semantically adequate paraphrase may support conversation yet be unusable as a quotation or grammar diagnostic. Conversely, one harmless spacing difference can raise a string metric while preserving meaning. Choose a configuration based on original-language fidelity and the app's downstream errors, not on English-looking transcripts.

## Evidence gate for later work

Research supports a testable English-output constraint because it implements the explicit preference, a separate original-language transcript/evidence path, and a paired representative ASR experiment. It does not yet support an empirical claim that the shipped prompt always complies, that automatic detection is more accurate than an English hint, or that either language policy produces better long-term speaking outcomes.

Before an ASR configuration is called validated, retain the recordings/references within their authorized handling, frozen settings, raw outputs, scoring rules, disagreements, and paired results. Before a prompt is called validated, retain observed audio behavior on the conflicting-Korean, quotation, safety, and context-transition cases. Subsequent design should state remaining policy choices—especially treatment of spoken quotations versus proper names—without changing the learner's English-output preference or rewriting original transcripts.
