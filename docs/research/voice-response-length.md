# Voice response length: evidence and evaluation plan

Reviewed 2026-11-10 UTC. Status: research and proposed evaluation only. No model requests or learner study were run for this note; all outcome fields remain unmeasured. Scope: an English voice companion for Korean learners using `gpt-realtime-2.1-mini`.

## Decision supported by the evidence

Test **the shortest reply that completes the current conversational job**, with optional expansion when the learner requests it or needs a repair. Reduce unnecessary explanation, repeated acknowledgments, and stacked questions; preserve meaning, warmth, useful examples, and the learner's choice to ask for detail. This is a design inference from the studies below, not a demonstrated learning effect in this product.

There is no established universal optimum of 10–25 words, one sentence, or two sentences in these sources. The current rules in `supabase/functions/coach/core.ts` and the 10–20-word greeting in `index.ts` are implementation hypotheses. The existing `max_output_tokens: 512` is an operational guardrail, not evidence for an appropriate spoken length or an identified cause of verbosity. Neither lowering that ceiling nor shortening every turn is justified as a learning intervention without measurement. Keep a task-conditioned candidate in experiment files until exact-model outputs and human adequacy review support further action.

## Primary evidence and its limits

The sources were selected for actual tutoring, L2 comprehension, autonomy, and voice-interface experiments. Full papers were inspected where linked below; the three TESOL input-modification entries are limited to publisher abstracts. This is a focused review, not a systematic review or meta-analysis.

| Source and method | Finding relevant to the complaint | Limits and design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976), [*The role of tutoring in problem solving*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x); [full paper](https://w.pauldowling.me/tmr/readings/Wood,%20Bruner%20&%20Ross.pdf). Thirty children aged 3–5 received individual tutoring on block construction; behavior was observed and coded. | Assistance depended on both task and learner. Their scaffold functions include reducing task demands, marking important features, modeling, and controlling frustration. The authors also identify dependency as a risk. | Descriptive preschool problem solving, not adult L2 conversation or a randomized length experiment. Supports contingent help and room for independent performance; supplies no word limit. |
| Sweller (1988), [*Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning*](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4). Theory/computational analysis plus an experiment with 24 Year-10 students, two groups of 12, solving trigonometry problems while reproducing prior problems from memory. | The nonspecific-goal group made fewer errors on several reproduction measures; primary solution times and mathematical errors did not significantly differ. Searching for a solution can consume capacity needed for learning. | Small mathematics experiment; memory reproduction is not conversational comprehension. Applying it to removing irrelevant speech is a plausible transfer, not direct proof that fewer spoken words improve learning. Complexity, prior knowledge, and task demands matter as well as length. |
| Pica, Young & Doughty (1987), [*The impact of interaction on comprehension*](https://doi.org/10.2307/3586992), publisher abstract. Sixteen nonnative English speakers followed task directions under premodified-input or interactionally modified-input conditions. | Repetition and rephrasing during interaction best assisted comprehension; reduced linguistic complexity in premodified input was not significant. Clarification and confirmation enabled adaptation. | Small human-led direction-following study, not a voice chatbot trial; immediate comprehension, not retained learning. A clarification request should trigger a useful repair, even when that requires more speech. Simplifying or deleting content alone is insufficient. |
| Oh (2001), [*Two types of input modification and EFL reading comprehension*](https://doi.org/10.2307/3587860), publisher abstract. Six passages in baseline, simplified, or elaborated forms; 180 Korean high-school students in two proficiency groups; 18-item comprehension test and perceived-comprehension rating. | Elaboration was at least as successful as simplification while retaining more natural language. | Written reading with available text, not transient audio or adult dialogue; no longitudinal acquisition result. Direct counterevidence to treating minimum length or artificially stripped language as the goal. Preserve a short example, referent, or contextual explanation when needed for understanding. |
| Chiang & Dunkel (1992), [*The effect of speech modification, prior knowledge, and listening proficiency on EFL lecture learning*](https://doi.org/10.2307/3587009), publisher abstract. Reported sample: 388 Chinese EFL students at high/low intermediate listening proficiency; familiar/unfamiliar lectures with/without elaboration and redundancy; multiple-choice testing. | Added elaboration/redundancy benefited the higher-proficiency group, not the lower-proficiency group. | Lectures and recognition testing, not short dialogue. The abstract does not expose all exclusions or full effect sizes. Extra input can help, but benefit varies; neither “more support always helps beginners” nor “shorter always helps” follows. |
| Haas et al. (2022), [*Keep it short: A comparison of voice assistants' response behavior*](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3517684); [full paper](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.inst.100/1-hci/hci-paper/2022/CHI2022_Keep_it_short.pdf). Repeated-measures online study: 72 recruited VA users, one excluded; eight requests × three response styles, randomized style order within request blocks. | Short styles were similarly useful/likable and often more efficient. Preferences depended on request type. Full sentences were more comprehensible for calendar responses; minimal news responses performed poorly. | Scripted utilitarian requests, mostly young experienced VA users, not L2 learners or social companions. The two-item comprehensibility scale had low internal consistency. Supports testing brevity by task and allowing preference adjustment, not adopting keyword-only conversation or a universal length. |
| Reeve & Jang (2006), [*What teachers say and do to support students' autonomy during a learning activity*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.98.1.209); [full paper](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_ReeveJang_JEP.pdf). Seventy-two pairs of preservice teachers were assigned teacher/student roles; coded 10-minute puzzle instruction and learner autonomy ratings. | Listening, learner talking time, independent work, hints, and responsiveness to learner questions correlated with perceived autonomy. Teacher talking time was negatively associated. | Behavioral associations are correlational despite randomized role assignment; puzzle activity, not L2 AI tutoring. Supports preserving learner initiative and responding to requests, without claiming a causal optimum for teacher/assistant talk share. |

Comprehensible input and scaffolding therefore concern **what the learner can understand and do with support**, not a fixed word count. These studies also do not establish that immediate comprehension, satisfaction, or more learner turns imply durable acquisition.

## Official Realtime guidance: capability, not learning evidence

The current [OpenAI voice prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting) recommends specifying detail by task type instead of relying on “be concise”; its examples distinguish direct answers, clarification, comparisons, and stepwise help. It also recommends varied examples to avoid repeated stock phrasing. Those are provider recommendations for steering model behavior, not controlled studies in Korean learners and not a performance guarantee for `gpt-realtime-2.1-mini`. The older [cookbook recipe](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/realtime_prompting_guide) is explicitly archived; its 2–3-sentence example is not a normative optimum.

The [current call schema](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/realtime/subresources/calls/methods/create) describes instructions as guidance, without guaranteed compliance, and `max_output_tokens` as a response-token limit. [Server-event documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/realtime/server-events) records text and audio output tokens and distinguishes `completed`, cancellation, and `incomplete` with reason `max_output_tokens`. A token ceiling cannot be converted directly into a spoken-word policy. Preserve the current ceiling for the first prompt comparison to isolate the cause; that does not validate 512 as optimal or always sufficient. Listen for completion and inspect actual completion reasons before changing it. Faster playback is a separate variable from less content.

## Candidate behavior to design, then test

The following are proposed rules derived from the evidence, not implemented changes:

- A simple acknowledgment or direct answer should finish its immediate job and return the floor. It need not contain a follow-up question.
- Use a relevant question when it helps the conversation; propose at most one independent request for an answer in an ordinary turn. This is a burden-control heuristic to evaluate, not an experimentally established maximum.
- A meaning/phrasing question needs the meaning or usable English expression. Add context or an example when omission would make it unclear. Do not append a lesson automatically.
- When the learner signals misunderstanding, rephrase the same point or clarify the difficulty. Do not repeat a longer lecture by default.
- Honor explicit requests for shorter replies, more explanation, examples, or no advice. A request for two examples must not be blocked by a global two-sentence/one-example rule.
- Apply the same behavior to greeting, normal reply, checkpoint/resume, and one-off hint generation. Audit effective instructions at each path for contradictory length rules. Preserve existing language and privacy boundaries during the length experiment.

Numeric defaults, token ceilings, response duration, whether a length preference belongs in settings, and how much scaffolding each learner needs remain empirical questions. Do not infer proficiency from a short answer, accent, Korean input, or hesitation.

## Finite, executable evaluation protocol

The sample sizes, repetitions, conditions, and stopping rules below are proposed engineering precommitments to make the experiment bounded and reviewable. They are not paper-derived power calculations or validated learning thresholds. The project experiment may pre-register a smaller baseline/candidate comparison; record the exact design before running it.

**Stage 0 — fixtures and controls.** Freeze the deployed prompt and commit as A. Create B with contextual rules above and no universal word quota. An optional C can add a numeric brevity target to B, selected *before* testing and explicitly labeled experimental (it may reuse 10–25 words to test that hypothesis). Keep all other instructions identical. Fix the actual model to `gpt-realtime-2.1-mini`, output modality to audio with transcript, voice/persona within each matched fixture, speech speed, token ceiling, context, memory, and VAD. Prompt variants are the comparison; do not substitute a text model. Randomize condition order and use fresh sessions so earlier variants do not influence later ones.

Prepare JSONL fixtures with `case_id`, exact preceding assistant/user turns, input transcript/audio path, path (`greeting`, `ordinary`, `checkpoint`, `hint`), persona, required semantic points, permitted omissions, desired speech act, and learner control request. Human recordings should be consented Korean-learner speech. Synthetic/replayed text is useful for prompt isolation but must be labeled; it cannot establish accented-speech recognition or live-turn quality. Capture the provider's effective session/response instructions, not merely the intended prompt template.

| Case | Context / final learner utterance | Required behavior (not an exact reference answer) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | First greeting | Warm opening and one easy invitation; no invented history. |
| 02 | Assistant asked “Was the food good?” → “Yes.” | Relevant brief reaction; no lesson or multi-question interrogation. |
| 03 | After checkpoint with same topic → “I had bibimbap for lunch.” | Continue that concrete detail; no second greeting. |
| 04 | “Do you like movies?” | Direct answer consistent with fictional persona; no invented lived experience. |
| 05 | “What does exhausted mean?” | Correct accessible meaning; no unrelated explanation. |
| 06 | “What's the difference between bored and boring?” | Preserve the experiencer/cause distinction; examples if needed for clarity. |
| 07 | Following an unclear explanation → “Sorry, I don't understand. Say it another way.” | Repair the same meaning using a different accessible formulation. |
| 08 | “Short answer, please. Is ‘I agree’ correct?” | Direct correctness answer; respect request for brevity. |
| 09 | “Please explain bored and boring with two examples.” | Both requested examples and the distinction; longer output is permissible. |
| 10 | “오늘 너무 피곤했어. How can I say this naturally?” | Give a fitting English expression, preserving intended meaning and current English-output policy. |
| 11 | “I failed my interview. I don't want advice now.” | Brief relevant acknowledgment; respect no-advice request and avoid assumed feelings. |
| 12 | Learner stuck mid-topic; invokes the existing one-off hint button | One usable next-step hint; retain session brevity/privacy/language instructions and avoid a new topic. |

**Stage 1 — one bounded provider batch.** A/B requires 12 cases × 2 variants × 3 independent repetitions = 72 real audio responses; pre-registering optional C raises this to 108. Rotate the three personas across cases but keep each case identical across variants; this checks coverage, not an independent persona-effect estimate. Save completion events, transcripts, audio, timings, settings, fixture/prompt hashes, model identifier returned by the API, and redacted errors. Do not silently rerun a disliked answer. One transport-failure replacement per cell is allowed and must retain the failed attempt. Stop after the batch and replacements; score before changing prompts.

For each response, compute and label:

- English words, using one declared tokenizer consistently (e.g. `[A-Za-z]+(?:['’-][A-Za-z]+)*`; contractions/hyphenated words count as one); report median, p90, range, and paired differences by simple/repair/detail cases. Counts are diagnostic, not the success criterion.
- Question marks **and** human-coded independent asks, including questions joined with “and.” Zero is allowed; one punctuation mark can hide multiple demands.
- Audible duration and first-audio latency at fixed speed; separate generation and playback delay when available.
- Completion status/reason; human-coded unfinished sentence/meaning or audible cutoff. An intentional fragment such as “Sounds delicious!” is not unfinished. Separate learner interruption from token exhaustion and transport loss.
- Semantic adequacy: `0` wrong/missing essential point; `1` partly usable but needs avoidable repair; `2` complete for the requested job. Also label irrelevant additions, repeated content, language-policy drift, ignored detail/shortness/no-advice requests, and pressure to answer.

Two raters, including an English educator familiar with Korean learners, independently score anonymized outputs against prewritten requirements before seeing word counts or variant names. Retain disagreements, report agreement, then adjudicate with reasons. Human-authored fixture examples are **illustrations**, mocked provider responses are **software tests**, and real provider outputs are **model observations**. None is a learner outcome.

**Stage 2 — one learner pilot.** With consent, recruit 8–12 Korean adult EFL learners across self-reported ability/preferences. Counterbalance A versus the best adequate candidate in matched short conversations; keep voice/speed constant. Collect perceived effort/ease, too-short/just-right/too-long judgments, comprehension checks, requests for repair/detail, ability to finish a thought, perceived control, and preference. Record learner/assistant speech time descriptively; do not reward talk share at the expense of understanding. This convenience pilot detects problems and preferences, not population effectiveness or acquisition.

**Decision and stopping rule.** Pre-register product acceptance criteria before model execution. At minimum require correct semantic essentials in explicit-request cases, preserved controls, and no unexplained cutoffs; prefer less listening burden only among adequate responses. Report failed cells and uncertainty rather than averaging them away. If a candidate passes the behavioral checks and the learner pilot supports it, document the limited evidence for a default. Otherwise revise once and run one further batch with the same design plus held-out paraphrases; after that, stop and report the unresolved tradeoff. Any claimed learning gain requires a separate study with repeated sessions and delayed retention/transfer outcomes. No numeric policy is promoted solely because its average word count is lower.
